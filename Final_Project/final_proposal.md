## What I'm Building

The Campus Advocacy Toolkit is a fully self-contained, school-facing web application designed to serve as the operational bridge between four of the most impactful artist-founded nonprofits working with young people today — The Busyhead Project, The Ally Coalition, The Midwest Princess Project, and The Laufey Foundation — and the high schools and colleges that stand to benefit most from their missions. This is not a brochure or a one-page resource list. It is a living, functional platform that a school counselor, GSA advisor, music director, or student affairs coordinator can open on any device and immediately begin using to understand each foundation, access materials tailored to their specific campus community, generate professional outreach correspondence, and track their school's integration progress over time. The core design philosophy is that schools should be empowered to advocate for themselves using these foundations as tools, rather than waiting passively for an outreach coordinator to hand-deliver every resource to them individually.
## Why I Chose This

When I began thinking seriously about how to integrate these four foundations into educational institutions at scale, the first thing I recognized was a structural problem that no amount of individual effort could fully solve. Outreach done manually — one customized email at a time, one follow-up conversation at a time, one resource packet assembled per school — creates a pipeline that is entirely dependent on the coordinator being present and active at every single stage. The moment capacity shrinks, the work stalls. Schools that seemed interested lose momentum. Foundations never hear back. Students who needed those resources most never encounter them at all.
What makes this particularly urgent is that the gap isn't a resources problem — all four of these foundations have genuinely exceptional materials, missions, and support structures already in place. The gap is a discovery and activation problem. A student experiencing a mental health crisis at a school in rural Ohio may never know that The Busyhead Project exists and has community organizations in their region ready to help. A trans student at a community college may not know that the Midwest Princess Project has a trans-inclusive policy checklist their administration could adopt tomorrow. A talented young musician from a low-income household may miss a Laufey Foundation grant deadline simply because no one at their school knew the foundation existed. The problem isn't supply — it's connection.
This app is my answer to that problem. By building something that schools can receive, keep, and use autonomously, I'm converting what would otherwise be a temporary outreach moment into a permanent institutional resource. I'm also making the work of advocacy feel approachable — because right now, for a lot of school administrators, "partnering with a nonprofit" sounds like a multi-month process involving paperwork, approvals, and budget conversations. This tool breaks it down into something a school can begin today, whether that means reading about a foundation's mission, downloading a workshop guide, or generating a letter to their own principal requesting that one of these programs be brought onto campus.
I chose these four foundations specifically because together they cover the three most urgent and underserved areas of student wellbeing in American schools right now: mental health, LGBTQ+ inclusion and safety, and access to arts education. The Busyhead Project and The Ally Coalition and the Midwest Princess Project together address the emotional, social, and identity-based challenges that are driving some of the most alarming youth health statistics we've seen in a generation. The Laufey Foundation addresses something equally critical but chronically treated as expendable — the belief that access to music education and creative development is not a luxury but a genuine pathway to student success and self-discovery. Combining all four into a single platform also creates a multiplier effect, where a school that comes in searching for mental health resources discovers they can simultaneously build a safer environment for LGBTQ+ students and launch a music scholarship program. The whole becomes far greater than the sum of its parts.

## Core Features

The Overview dashboard is the entry point of the application and is designed to accomplish one specific thing: make a school stakeholder who has never heard of any of these organizations feel oriented, informed, and genuinely motivated within the first two minutes of using the app. Each of the four foundations gets a dedicated card displaying their founder, their core mission, key impact statistics, and a visual identity that reflects the personality of each organization. Live pipeline statistics at the top of the page would show how many schools are currently in the outreach cycle and how many have become active partners, giving the tool a sense of shared momentum that a static PDF simply cannot replicate.
The Resource Toolkit is the most substantive section of the application and the one schools will return to most frequently after their initial introduction. Resources are filtered by foundation so that the right person — a counselor, a faculty advisor, a department head, a student leader — lands immediately on content relevant to their specific role and need. For The Busyhead Project this would include things like a Mental Health 101 Workshop Guide for classroom facilitation, a Campus Resource Mapping Template to help students identify local mental health services, a Peer Ambassador Training Outline for volunteer student advocates, a Social Media Awareness Kit for Mental Health Awareness Month, Story Sharing Guidelines for students who want to share their own journeys safely, and a Community Wall Activation Kit inspired by the fan engagement model Busyhead deployed on Noah Kahan's tours. For The Ally Coalition the toolkit would include a GSA Starter Pack, an Inclusive Curriculum Add-On guide for teachers, an Allyship Training Script for faculty and student sessions, a Campus Climate Survey Template, Advocacy Letter Templates for students to send to administration, and Safe Space Signage materials. For the Midwest Princess Project the resources would include a Trans Student Support Resource Guide covering legal rights and campus policy, a Trans-Inclusive Policy Checklist for administrators, a Visibility Event Planning Guide for Trans Awareness Week and Day of Visibility programming, a Healthcare Navigation Resource helping trans students find affirming local providers, a Student Storytelling Campaign Kit, and a Faculty Allyship Pledge toolkit. For The Laufey Foundation the toolkit would offer a Youth Orchestra Program Finder by region, an Instrument Lending Program setup guide, a Music Scholarship Application Guide walking students through the grant process, a Jazz and Classical Workshop Curriculum for new audiences, an Artist Mentorship Program framework, and a Campus Concert Series template for student-run fundraising events.
The Outreach Letter Builder is the feature most likely to save meaningful time for both our team and the schools themselves. A school representative would fill out a simple form capturing their institution's name, school type, the administrator they're writing to, their own name and role, and an open field describing anything specific about their campus community — an existing mental health program they want to build on, a strong arts department looking for grant connections, a recent incident that has made LGBTQ+ inclusion a priority. They then select which of the four foundations they want to include, and the application generates a complete, professionally written outreach letter tailored to that school's context. The letter is ready to send immediately with no editing required, dramatically lowering the barrier between awareness and action.
The School Pipeline Tracker functions as a lightweight relationship management system built specifically for this project. Every school in the outreach cycle — contacted, responding, or actively partnered — lives here with its status, assigned foundations, contact name, and running notes. Status updates happen directly in the tracker and the data flows back to the Overview dashboard in real time, giving leadership a live read on program health without requiring a separate report or manual update.

## What I Don't Know Yet

The most significant unknown is formal foundation approval. Everything in this proposal — the resource descriptions, the toolkit materials, the mission framing, the statistics — has been developed using publicly available information about each organization. But none of it has been reviewed or endorsed by The Busyhead Project, The Ally Coalition, the Midwest Princess Project, or The Laufey Foundation directly. Before this app is distributed to schools under any of those names, there needs to be a clear process for legal and communications review from each foundation's team. Some may want to contribute their own materials. Others may have restrictions on how their branding or programming language is used in third-party tools. This is the step that determines whether this is a legitimate co-branded partnership or an unauthorized use of their identity, and I don't yet know what that approval pathway looks like for any of the four organizations.
The second major unknown is data persistence and technical infrastructure. A truly useful version of this app — one where schools save their progress, our team's tracker survives across sessions, and usage data informs future development — requires backend infrastructure that hasn't been scoped yet. That means deciding between options like browser-based local storage, a cloud database, a shared spreadsheet integration, or a full account-based system, each of which carries different cost, complexity, and maintenance implications. I haven't made that architectural decision and it will meaningfully shape how the app is built.
Third, I don't yet know the right level of personalization by school type and size. Right now the concept treats every user the same — whether they're a principal at a 4,000-student suburban high school or a single student affairs coordinator at a small rural community college. In practice those two users have entirely different constraints, vocabularies, approval chains, and student populations. Building adaptive content that responds intelligently to school context is possible but it represents a significant design and development investment that needs to be scoped and prioritized deliberately.
Fourth, I haven't defined what a successful integration actually looks like in measurable terms. We can track whether a school became an active partner, but we currently have no way to capture whether students at that school actually used the resources, whether a workshop was held, how many students attended, or what the downstream effect was on mental health outcomes, LGBTQ+ safety climate surveys, or music program enrollment. Without an impact reporting layer, we can show activity but we cannot demonstrate outcomes — and outcomes are what foundations, administrators, and future funding sources ultimately care about. Designing that measurement framework is a major open question.
Finally, there are content maintenance questions I haven't resolved. Each of these four foundations is actively evolving — new grant cycles open, new partner organizations come on board, statistics get updated, programming focus shifts. The resource content in this app will become outdated without a clear ownership and update process. Who reviews it, how often, and how schools that already have a copy of the tool receive those updates are all operational questions that need answers before this becomes something we can responsibly distribute as a long-term institutional resource.
